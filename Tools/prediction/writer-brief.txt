Brief given to each of ten writers (language-model subagents) to produce the test passages.
Each writer got a different theme, voice, and list of seed words, so the set is varied and no two
writers fall into the same phrasing. The seed words were drawn at random from the middle of the
frequency dictionary (ranks 2,500 to 22,000, 5 to 11 letters).

Themes, one per writer:
  00 workplace emails to a manager and teammates on a software team
  01 text messages to close friends (weekend plans, what happened last night)
  02 private journal entries about health and routine
  03 messages a customer writes to support teams and shops
  04 a software engineer's working notes and bug reports, in plain prose
  05 student life: emails to a professor, study notes, job applications
  06 family and household: relatives, a landlord, car repair, bills
  07 short reviews and recommendations
  08 meeting notes and status updates in the third person
  09 storytelling and opinions written to a forum or a friend

The brief (theme, seed words and file number filled in per writer):

Write 30 short passages of everyday typed English and save them as one JSON Lines file. They are
test data for a typing assistant (next-word prediction, verb tense, spelling).

Each line is one JSON object:
  "id"        "NN-01" through "NN-30"
  "register"  two or three words describing the kind of writing
  "tense"     one of "past", "present", "future", "mixed"
  "text"      the passage, correctly spelled: 3 to 5 sentences, 35 to 80 words
  "typed"     the same passage as a fast typist with dyslexia might type it: identical to "text"
              except for 3 to 5 misspelled words
  "fixes"     a list of [wrong, right] pairs, one per misspelled word, in order

Requirements:
- Tense mix: 10 past, 8 present, 6 future, 6 mixed (the time clearly shifts inside the passage).
  Within past, present and future passages the tense stays consistent across every sentence,
  because the test checks whether a predictor follows the tense set by earlier sentences.
- Many different verbs, regular and irregular. Do not reuse the same opener or verb more than a
  few times.
- Write like a real person typing: natural, specific, a little uneven; vary sentence length.
- Plain ASCII only: straight quotes, no long dashes, no emojis, no lists, no line breaks.
- Misspellings realistic for dyslexia: phonetic spellings, swapped letters, dropped or doubled
  letters, wrong vowels. At most one passage in five may use a real-word mix-up (their/there).
  Never change punctuation, capitals at sentence starts, or word order.
- Use one or two of the seed words per passage, naturally, rotating through all of them.
- Invented first names only. No real people, addresses, phone numbers or emails.
- Do not copy text from anywhere. Avoid stock phrases and avoid sounding like an AI assistant.

Long documents (documents-dev.jsonl, documents-test.jsonl)

A second set, written later by ten more writers (20 to 29), to test what the short passages cannot:
whether the predictor follows the subject and the vocabulary of a longer text. Each writer got a
theme and seed words as above and wrote ten documents of 200 to 320 words in two to four
paragraphs, each about one specific thing whose terms come back through the document. No
misspelled copy; these are for prediction only. Themes:
  20 engineering design notes and code review comments in prose
  21 long emails to a team or manager about status and decisions, in many kinds of workplace
  22 long chat messages to coworkers explaining a problem and what happens next
  23 personal emails to friends and relatives
  24 writing about oneself: cover letters, self-reviews, application answers
  25 how-to explanations written for a forum or a friend
  26 meeting recaps and weekly updates, mostly third person
  27 journal entries about hobbies and ordinary days
  28 a customer writing at length: complaints, requests, reviews
  29 short essays and opinions on everyday subjects
Even-numbered writers are the tuning half, odd-numbered the test half (merge_documents.py).

One writer over three weeks (writers/w00.jsonl to w19.jsonl)

A third set, to test what neither of the others can: whether the predictor learns one writer's
recurring working vocabulary over time. Twenty writers, each one specific person in one job, each
wrote 60 texts of 15 to 60 words in the order they would have been typed over about three weeks.
The same handful of projects, systems, tools, tasks and colleagues come back through a writer's
texts while new subjects appear and old ones fade, and about a third of the texts are ordinary talk
(scheduling, thanks, lunch). Correct spelling, plain ASCII, invented names only. One JSON object
per line with a "text". Jobs, in file order:
  00 backend engineer who maintains data pipelines and SQL reports
  01 hospital ward nurse
  02 frontend web engineer on a design-system team
  03 paralegal at a small litigation firm
  04 site reliability engineer who is often on call
  05 high school science teacher
  06 data analyst at a retail company
  07 bookkeeper for several small businesses
  08 QA engineer testing a mobile app
  09 kitchen manager at a busy restaurant
  10 machine learning engineer training ranking models
  11 B2B sales representative selling logistics software
  12 engineering manager of a platform team
  13 biology graduate student running lab experiments
  14 IT helpdesk administrator at a mid-size office
  15 construction site supervisor
  16 technical writer documenting an API product
  17 customer support agent for a subscription service
  18 product manager for a payments feature
  19 property manager for apartment buildings
Writers 00 to 09 are for tuning, 10 to 19 are scored only.
